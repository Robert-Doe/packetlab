Module 19 — Exam Practice

Q1. capstone_checker.py reports 100% completeness. Does this mean your capstone is done, in a full quality sense? Why or why not?
A: No -- the brief explicitly states 100% is "a floor, not a ceiling." The checker verifies INTERNAL CONSISTENCY: that claimed firewall rules match claimed isolation, that findings are remediated, that the report meets a minimum length. It cannot verify whether your network design is actually SOUND, whether your remediation was the right one, or whether your report is genuinely well-reasoned -- those require human (or self-) judgment the checker isn't built to make.
Q2. Why does the capstone brief insist on reconstructing the simulated incident forensically "even if you watched it happen live"?
A: A real incident responder analyzing an actual breach is virtually never present when it happened -- they have only whatever evidence their tooling captured. If your reconstruction secretly relies on memory of watching the attack happen rather than what your logs/SIEM/ capture actually show, that's a real, hidden gap in your monitoring setup that this exercise is specifically designed to surface -- it forces your evidence-gathering to stand on its own, the actual standard real forensic work must meet.
Q3. The brief requires "at least 2 isolated segments with a real, working firewall rule enforcing the isolation (verified with an actual ping test, not just asserted)." Why does this specific phrasing matter?
A: "Asserted" isolation means simply declaring or configuring a rule and assuming it works; "verified with an actual ping test" means empirically confirming the rule has the intended effect by attempting the traffic it should block and observing the failure. This distinction matters because firewall rules are a common source of silent misconfiguration (wrong interface, wrong direction, rule ordering issues) -- the same "test what you built, don't just trust the configuration" discipline this entire course has modeled from Module 01 onward (e.g., Module 05's routing bug that was only caught by actually running the simulation, not by reading the code).
Q4. Requirement 3 asks for a real vulnerability finding AND a remediation you "actually applied (not just documented as 'should fix')." Why does the checker specifically flag unremediated findings as a gap?
A: A vulnerability assessment that only produces a list of problems, without follow-through, has real value only as a starting point -- the actual security benefit comes from FIXING the problem, not from knowing about it. A capstone that stopped at "found a finding" would demonstrate scanning skill but not the complete assess-and-remediate cycle that real security work requires; flagging an unremediated finding as an explicit gap enforces that the capstone demonstrates the whole cycle, not just its first half.
Q5. Why does this module claim "there's no new mechanism to learn here" -- is that actually true, given the amount of work the capstone requires?
A: Yes, in the specific sense that every individual TECHNIQUE required (VLSM planning, firewall rules, detector logic, SIEM pipelines, vulnerability scanning, forensic reconstruction, incident reporting) was already taught and tested in Modules 04 through 18. What's new is the INTEGRATION work -- making all of these pieces function together as one coherent, internally consistent system, which is a genuinely different skill from executing any one piece in isolation, and is exactly the skill a real security role (and a comprehensive exam) actually demands.
Q6. Why does the tutorial insist on explaining your build "out loud... without notes" as the final step, rather than considering the working system itself sufficient proof of mastery?
A: Being able to build something (possibly by following patterns, copying structure, or trial-and-error) is a different skill from being able to articulate WHY each decision was made, fluently and under scrutiny, in the field's own vocabulary. A comprehensive exam specifically tests the latter -- the ability to reason and explain live, not just to have produced a working artifact. This final step is the actual rehearsal for that exact situation.
Q7. Synthesis: name three DIFFERENT modules whose core mechanism you would need to correctly explain, unscripted, to defend a capstone network with 2 segments, a detected port scan, and a remediated CVE finding.
A: Any coherent answer should include at minimum: Module 04/09 (VLSM subnetting to actually plan the 2 segments), Module 10 (the firewall rule mechanism -- stateful filtering/conntrack -- enforcing the isolation between them), Module 14 (the statistical reasoning behind the port-scan detector, e.g., tight timing window across many distinct ports from one source), and Module 16 (CVSS-based severity reasoning and the CVE/remediation cycle for the vulnerability finding). A strong answer explains not just WHAT each module's tool does, but WHY its underlying mechanism produces the specific evidence or protection claimed -- exactly the standard this course has built toward from Module 01 onward.
